<intro>Should I file mandamus for a delayed EB-5 petition is a fact-specific decision under 28 U.S.C. section 1361. A long wait can raise the question of whether a lawsuit will get USCIS to act. A mandamus lawsuit may be an option in some cases. It is not an automatic next step. The useful question is whether the delay is unreasonable under the circumstances, and whether litigation makes sense for that petition.</intro>

Mandamus is a federal court action asking the government to perform a duty it has not completed. In an immigration case, that generally means asking the court to require the agency to adjudicate a petition. It does not ask the court to approve it. USCIS may approve the petition, deny it, issue a request for evidence, or take another action. Filing suit does not guarantee a favorable decision or a specific timeline.

## How an attorney assesses the delay

USCIS processing-time estimates can be a useful reference, but they do not tell the whole story. An attorney may also consider the petition type, how long comparable cases have been pending, recent adjudication patterns, and the facts of the case. These conditions change. A decision that seemed premature several months ago may warrant a fresh discussion now, or the reverse may be true.

The project can matter as well. Counsel may want to know whether USCIS has acted on the project filing, whether other investors in the same project have received decisions, and whether anything about the project could affect the case. A delay should be evaluated against the full record, not just a number on a processing-time page.

## What happens after a mandamus lawsuit is filed

Some investors file individually. In other circumstances, investors may join a group lawsuit. The appropriate approach depends on the legal and factual details. Once a case is filed, the government has an opportunity to respond. It may agree to a schedule, request more time, contest the lawsuit, or take action on the underlying petition.

None of those possibilities guarantees approval. USCIS may adjudicate the petition while the lawsuit is pending, but adjudication is not the same as approval. The agency could issue a request for evidence or deny the petition. If a court dismisses the lawsuit, that does not automatically mean USCIS has denied the underlying petition. The petition may continue through the administrative process.

A mandamus case is not premium processing. It is litigation, with court procedures, legal fees, and uncertainty. Ask an attorney to explain the likely costs and possible outcomes before deciding to proceed.

## Review the petition before taking action

Before recommending a lawsuit, counsel should review the petition and supporting evidence. That includes the investment, source and path of funds, and relevant project documents. Be open with your attorney about how the investment funds were earned, transferred, and committed. If there are gaps or inconsistencies, you want to understand them before deciding whether to bring additional attention to the case.

Ask what facts support a claim of unreasonable delay. Ask how the project's status affects the analysis, whether the petition has issues USCIS may raise, and what could happen if the agency acts on the case after suit is filed. If source-of-funds documentation is part of the concern, the <a href="/research/ways-to-fund-eb5-investment-2026">EB-5 funding guide</a> offers general background. Your attorney still needs to assess your own records.

## Questions to ask before hiring mandamus counsel

Ask whether the attorney has handled federal immigration litigation and EB-5 cases like yours. Confirm what the quoted fee includes, including work on government responses, extensions, or motions. Ask what happens to the underlying petition if the lawsuit is dismissed or USCIS issues a request for evidence.

A lawsuit may be useful in some delayed cases. It is not a guaranteed shortcut, and the right timing depends on the individual facts. Get a case-specific assessment from an attorney experienced in mandamus litigation before making a decision.
